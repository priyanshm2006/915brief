Pre-Open Edition

Wednesday, 2 September 2026

What moved overnight, and how it reaches Indian markets. Before the bell.

Oil spikes, Wall Street slides again as US-Iran tensions escalate — GIFT Nifty points to a cautious open

What happened. The US struck Iranian targets overnight after Iran's Revolutionary Guard attempted attacks on commercial shipping in the Strait of Hormuz; Iran said it retaliated with missile and drone strikes on US bases in Jordan and Bahrain, most of which were intercepted. Brent crude jumped to roughly $95.3–95.5 a barrel and WTI to around $91, both sharply higher than the ~$85.76 settle logged a week ago. US stocks fell for a second straight session — the S&P 500 down 0.71% and the Nasdaq down 1.03% on Tuesday, after a 0.7% Dow decline on Monday — while Treasury yields kept climbing, a combination one US wire framed explicitly as a global bond selloff.

How this reaches India: Two separate channels carry this in, and they cut in different directions. First, oil: India imports roughly 85% of its crude, so a move from the mid-$80s to the mid-$90s inside a week directly worsens the import bill and the current account arithmetic — the classic pressure point for the rupee and for fuel-linked inflation. It also splits the read-across inside India's own energy complex rather than lifting or hitting it as a block: upstream names such as ONGC and Oil India get better realisations on a higher crude price, while refiners and marketers (IOC, BPCL, HPCL) face margin compression if retail pump prices don't move in step, and fuel-cost-sensitive names like the airlines carry the cost directly. Second, the bond selloff: yields rising alongside falling equities — rather than the more familiar flight-to-safety pattern where bonds rally as stocks fall — is the signature of an oil-driven inflation scare rather than a pure risk-off move, and that matters for India because rising US real yields make foreign portfolio allocation to emerging-market equities less attractive at the margin. That pressure typically shows up first in FII cash-market flows, before it shows up in growth data.

Nifty 50 closed Tuesday's session at 24,055.80, down marginally (−24.60 points, −0.10%). GIFT Nifty was indicating a soft open near 24,020–24,040 as of roughly 8:30am IST, down 35–54 points from its own prior close — see the Overnight Global Tape section below for why that implied gap should be read with more caution than usual this morning.

August closed with FIIs net sellers of the month on an NSE cash basis — but the month was actually positive through 27 August; the entire outflow came in the final two sessions (28 and 31 August), so a reader shown only the monthly total would infer a month of steady selling that didn't happen. Separately: at least one widely read outlet's pre-open piece today reports the 1 September FII figure as a net sale of ₹1,143.38 crore — the magnitude is right, the sign is inverted. Two independently sourced platforms, plus the fact that this figure equals September's month-to-date exactly (as it must on the month's first session), confirm the correct reading is a net buy. This is at least the fourth time since mid-August this desk has caught a flow figure published with an inverted sign, and it is the single figure worth checking twice before repeating.

GIFT Nifty's own premium to the confirmed Nifty spot close has run roughly 150–165 points through most of the past two weeks — ordinary cost of carry. This morning it has compressed to close to zero, even negative on some readings, which is unusual enough that this brief is not converting the reading into an implied opening level: a basis that has moved this much in one session is more likely to be measurement noise (or a genuine repricing of carry costs amid the risk-off tape) than a reliable anchor, and manufacturing a precise "implied open" from it would be false precision. The direction — down roughly a fifth to a fifth-and-a-half of a percent — is corroborated two ways and is the actual signal worth reading.
